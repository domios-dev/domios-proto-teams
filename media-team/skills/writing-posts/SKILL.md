# How we write posts

This is the approach we use for our own posts, with real numbers from our accounts. Run every draft through the humanizer skill before it goes out.

## The one rule

**Every post rests on something real:** a concrete event, a hard number, or a practical step-by-step fix for a real pain. No generic thought-leadership theses.

Our data across 21 LinkedIn posts over a year makes this plain:

| Views | What it was |
|---|---|
| 1,325 | a personal, specific moment ("Something shifted in December 2025…") |
| 1,294 | a real event with a known name ("Got a nice surprise from the Claude Code team…") |
| 704 | news plus an opinion; most comments |
| 607 | a hard number ("21,650 commits since March…") |
| 606 | a personal launch announcement |
| 85–137 | generic theses ("Agent infrastructure is becoming a product category", "The AI market is getting less generous") |

The median was 198 views and a hit was 600+. The theses landed at half the median. On Threads, a practical Russian guide to a real pain (agents on a VPS) got about 60× the views of the English opinion posts.

Before you write, ask: **what actually happened, what is the number, or what exact steps solve it?** If there is no answer, there is no post yet.

## LinkedIn structure

1. **Hook** of 6–8 words: a number, a bold claim, or a moment. It must make sense on its own above "…see more".
2. **Context** in 1–2 sentences: what happened and why it matters.
3. **The story or data.** Give one lived detail and the concrete numbers. Write as a practitioner: "I did X, here is what broke".
4. **The turn**, halfway through: the surprising part or the mistake.
5. **The insight**, woven into the story as a plain sentence. Never label it ("My take:", "Takeaway:", "Bottom line:").
6. **Ending.** Stop on the last concrete beat or one plain personal line. Do not end with a question to "the audience" ("Designers: …?", "How do you…?"). That is the most recognisable AI-post ending. Ask a question only rarely, when you truly want the answer and it is specific.
7. **3–5 hashtags** at the end.

Length: 1,000–2,000 characters. Short paragraphs. Links go in the **first comment**, never in the body, because a link in the body cuts reach.

## X and Threads

- **X.** 280 characters, and emoji count as 2. One sharp idea or a short how-to, with a number. Put the link in a reply.
- **Threads.** 500 characters per part, casual, closer to how you talk.
  - Threads are good for step-by-step guides: the pain, then numbered steps, then the link in the last part.
  - Native-language posts on local pains travel far.
- **Different text per platform.** Use the same story with a different hook and length, never a copy-paste.

## Voice (see the humanizer skill for the full list)

- Sound like a senior practitioner talking to peers, not a press release.
- Have opinions: "I think…", "this surprised me".
- Vary the rhythm: a short sentence, then a longer one.
- **Avoid:**
  - em dashes;
  - "it is not just X, it is Y";
  - rule-of-three lists;
  - corporate words such as leverage, delve, landscape or pivotal;
  - sycophantic openers;
  - emojis in the body.
- Never invent facts, numbers, quotes or reactions. If you don't know it, ask or leave it out.

## Cadence and tracking

- At most one post per day per network.
- Put every link on a short tracked link, so you can see which post actually brings visitors.
- Judge each post against your own baseline (the median views for that network), not against other people.

## Before publishing: checklist

- [ ] Is there a real event, number or how-to in the first two lines?
- [ ] Does the hook work alone, above the fold?
- [ ] Have you passed the humanizer check, including pattern 25: no labelled takeaway and no engagement-bait question at the end?
- [ ] Is the link in the first comment or reply, not the body?
- [ ] Is it within the platform length (X 280 with emoji as 2, Threads 500 per part)?
- [ ] Is every claim true and checkable?
